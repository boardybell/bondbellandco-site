The Adviser, the Business Manager, and the Agent

Insights · August 2026

Three professionals commonly sit around a household whose income comes from a career in technology, entertainment, or professional sports. An investment adviser. A business manager. An agent. The titles get used interchangeably in conversation. They should not be. Each role carries a different duty, is paid a different way, and sees a different slice of the household's life.

The adviser

An investment adviser is a legal category, not just a job description. In the United States the title refers to a person or firm registered with the Securities and Exchange Commission or with state securities regulators, and that registration carries a fiduciary duty. The duty is a standard imposed by law, not a slogan. An adviser must act in the client's interest, disclose conflicts, and put the client's interest ahead of its own. Advisers are typically compensated by advisory fees, often calculated on the assets they manage, and they describe their services, fees, and conflicts in a public filing called Form ADV. What an adviser sees is the balance sheet: the accounts, the positions, and the plan for the money once it exists.

The business manager

Business manager is not a regulated title. The role grew up around careers where money arrives in irregular shapes, and the work is operational. Paying bills. Moving cash between accounts. Keeping the books. Coordinating with tax preparers and handling the paperwork a working household generates. Many business managers are certified public accountants or work inside CPA firms, and their obligations flow mainly from that credential and from the terms of the engagement rather than from securities law. Compensation is commonly a percentage of the client's gross income, a flat retainer, or hourly billing. What a business manager sees is the cash: what came in, what went out, and what is due.

The agent

The agent is why the income exists. Agents negotiate the contracts, deals, and engagements that produce it, and they are usually paid a commission, a percentage of what they negotiate. Their duties come from agency law and from the engagement itself, and in some industries from guild, union, or league rules that govern who may represent talent and on what terms. What an agent sees is the future: the pipeline of work, and what the next deal could be.

Why the lines matter

None of this is a criticism of any role. Each arrangement fits the work it pays for. An agent paid on deals has every reason to find the next deal. A business manager paid on income has every reason to keep the operation running. An adviser paid on assets has every reason to attend to the assets.

The trouble is structural, not personal. The duties differ. What an adviser must do as a matter of law, a business manager may do as a matter of engagement, and an agent may have no occasion to consider at all. The compensation differs, so the attention differs. And the information differs, so each professional makes reasonable decisions from a partial picture. A contract negotiated in one office changes the cash managed in a second office and the plan built in a third, and nothing in any of the three engagements requires the news to travel.

The common failure is not a bad actor. It is a gap. No one is charged with holding the whole picture, so no one does. Households tend to discover the gap at the moments when the pieces have to fit together: a large deal, a year with unusual income, a career decision that changes what the next several years look like.

The useful questions are ordinary ones. Who is registered, and under what standard. How each professional is paid, in writing. Who is expected to talk to whom, and about what. No answer disqualifies anyone. But a household that can answer them knows where its whole picture is held, and where it is not.
