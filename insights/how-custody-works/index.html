How Custody Works

Insights · August 2026

Client assets do not sit with the adviser. They sit at a custodian. The arrangement is deliberate, it is regulated, and it is worth understanding before any other part of an advisory relationship.

What a custodian is

A qualified custodian is a regulated financial institution, most often a bank or a broker-dealer, that holds cash and securities on behalf of a client. The account is opened in the client's name. The assets in it belong to the client, not to the custodian and not to the adviser. Bond Bell & Co does not hold client assets. Client accounts are held at Schwab and Altruist.

The custodian is where the money actually lives. It settles the trades, collects the dividends and interest, processes the deposits and withdrawals, and keeps the ledger of everything the account holds and everything that has happened in it.

Why statements come from the custodian

Because the custodian keeps the ledger, the custodian issues the statements. Clients receive account statements at least quarterly, sent directly by the custodian rather than passed through the adviser. Each statement lists the holdings, their market values, and every transaction in the period, including any advisory fee that was deducted. Trade confirmations arrive the same way, directly from the custodian.

Why the accounts sit apart

The separation is the point. The adviser directs the account, deciding within the authority the client has granted what to buy and what to sell. The custodian holds the account and records what happens in it. Keeping those two roles at two different institutions means the record of the client's assets is maintained by a firm that is not the one being paid to manage them.

An adviser with trading authority can direct transactions inside the account. It does not take possession of the assets, and withdrawals from the account go to the client or to destinations the client has authorized with the custodian. Custody rules exist to hold that boundary in place, and much of what regulators examine in an advisory firm concerns exactly this boundary.

Fee deduction is a narrow exception

When a client authorizes the adviser's fee to be deducted from the account, regulators treat that authority as a limited form of custody. The rules that follow are specific. The deduction happens at the custodian, under the client's written authorization, and it appears as a line on the custodian's statement. The client sees the fee in the same document that shows everything else.

Read both documents

An adviser may also prepare its own reports, summaries of holdings and activity formatted for review and conversation. These are useful. They are also secondary. The custodian's statement is the primary record of the account, produced by the institution that holds the assets.

A careful client reads both and compares them. The holdings should match. The values should be close, allowing for pricing dates. The fees should be identical. If something on an adviser's report cannot be found on the custodian's statement, that is a question to ask, in writing, until the answer is clear.

The rules themselves encourage this habit. Advisers must reasonably believe the custodian sends statements directly to clients, and clients are urged to compare those statements against any report an adviser provides. The structure does not ask the client to take anyone's word for anything. That is what it is for.
