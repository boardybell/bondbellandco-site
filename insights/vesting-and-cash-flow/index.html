What a Vesting Schedule Does to Cash Flow

Insights · August 2026

Equity compensation is income with a calendar attached.

A salary arrives in even amounts at even intervals. Most household budgets are built on that rhythm, whether or not anyone drew them up as a plan. The mortgage, tuition, insurance, the ordinary run of monthly life: recurring costs sit comfortably on recurring income. The arithmetic stays simple because the timing stays smooth.

A grant of equity does not behave this way. The grant is a promise, and the promise vests on a schedule the employer wrote. Until a portion vests, it is not the holder's property. When it vests, it is. The schedule decides when, and the holder, in the ordinary case, does not.

How the schedule works

Schedules take a few common shapes. Some hold everything back for an initial period and then release the first portion all at once. That first release is called a cliff. After the cliff, further portions vest at intervals, monthly or quarterly or annually, until the grant is exhausted. Other schedules key vesting to events rather than dates: a sale of the company, a public listing, a performance milestone. The shapes differ, but the principle holds in every version. Income arrives when the schedule says so, in the amounts the schedule says, and not otherwise.

Vesting is also, in many cases, a taxable event. The rules differ by instrument, by election, and by circumstance, and the particulars belong with a household's own tax advisers. The general point can still be stated plainly. When equity vests, a tax obligation often arises at that moment, on that date, measured by what the shares are worth then. The holder did not pick the date. The calendar did.

Where salary logic fails

A budget built on salary rests on two quiet assumptions: that income is level, and that it is continuous. Vested equity honors neither. It arrives in steps rather than a stream. It pauses between vest dates. It is denominated in shares, whose value on the vest date is whatever it is. And the unvested remainder is ordinarily forfeited if employment ends, so the stream can stop midcalendar in a way a salary does not.

Read through salary logic, a vesting schedule invites familiar mistakes. Unvested amounts get counted as if already earned. Recurring commitments get set against income that arrives episodically. The vest date gets treated as a payday when it is also, often, a tax date. None of these is a failure of discipline. Each is a reasonable habit from one kind of income applied to another kind entirely.

The questions a schedule raises

The useful response is not a rule of thumb. It is a set of questions, put to the actual documents. When, exactly, does income arrive under this schedule, this year and the year after? What already stands committed against those dates, taxes among the possibilities? Which of the household's obligations have quietly come to depend on portions that have not vested? And how long does the schedule run: how short is the window in which this income arrives at all, and what is the household asking that window to finance?

These questions do not answer themselves, and the answers differ from one household to the next. The point of asking them is narrower and more durable. It is to read the calendar as it is written, rather than as a salary in disguise.
